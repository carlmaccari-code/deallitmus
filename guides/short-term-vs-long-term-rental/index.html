Short-term vs. long-term rental: the same house, both ways

One $320,000 house, one loan, two ways to rent it. The short-term version clears $173 a month before taxes, and the long-term version loses $391 a month.

Short-term rentals promise more income. Long-term rentals promise a quieter life. The fair comparison is one $320,000 house and one loan, run both ways, with every number shown. All figures are pre-tax.

The house and the loan

Both versions start from the same purchase:

  • Purchase price: $320,000
  • Down payment at 20%: $64,000
  • Loan: $256,000 at 7.25% for 30 years
  • Mortgage payment: $1,746 a month
  • Closing costs at 3%: $9,600

In this example the mortgage is the same either way. Only income and running costs differ.

Version one: short-term rental

The assumptions: a $185 average nightly rate, 60% occupancy, 15% of revenue to platform and management fees, and $950 a month in operating costs (property taxes, insurance, utilities, internet and supplies).

$185 × 365 nights × 60% = $40,515 a year, or $3,376 a month

Three bills come out of that monthly revenue:

  • Fees at 15%: about $506
  • Operating costs: $950
  • Mortgage: $1,746

Revenue minus fees minus operating costs minus mortgage = $173 a month

Adding the rounded lines by hand lands a dollar high. The calculator keeps the cents and reports $173 a month. Twelve months of $173 is $2,076. Thin, but positive.

The extra cash for furnishing

Guests expect beds, a stocked kitchen and linens before the first booking. This example budgets $18,000 for furnishing and setup, on top of the down payment and closing costs.

$64,000 + $9,600 + $18,000 = $91,600 cash needed

Earning $2,076 a year on $91,600 is a cash-on-cash return of 2.3%.

Break-even occupancy

Two bills arrive whether or not anyone books: operating costs and the mortgage. That is $950 + $1,746 = $2,696 a month. Break-even occupancy is the share of nights you must fill so revenue after fees covers those bills. A fully booked month would bring in about $4,783 after fees.

$2,696 ÷ $4,783 = 56%

The plan assumes 60%, so the cushion is about 4 percentage points. One slow season can use that up.

Version two: long-term rental

Same house, one tenant, $2,300 a month. The assumptions: 5% vacancy, $3,600 a year in property taxes, $1,500 a year in insurance, 5% of rent each for maintenance and CapEx, and 8% of collected rent for management.

  • Gross rent: $2,300 × 12 = $27,600 a year
  • After 5% vacancy: $26,220
  • Operating expenses: $3,600 + $1,500 + $2,760 for maintenance and CapEx + $2,098 for management = $9,958
  • Net operating income (NOI): $26,220 minus $9,958 = $16,262
  • Mortgage: $1,746 × 12 = $20,952 a year

The NOI does not cover the mortgage: about $1,355 a month against a $1,746 payment.

$1,746 mortgage minus $1,355 NOI = $391 short every month

Plainly: as a long-term rental, this house loses $391 a month. With the cents kept, the yearly loss is $4,694. On the $73,600 of cash needed ($64,000 + $9,600), that is a cash-on-cash return of −6.4%.

DSCR = NOI ÷ annual mortgage payments = $16,262 ÷ $20,952 = 0.78

A DSCR (debt service coverage ratio) below 1 means the property's income does not cover its loan payments. Lenders that qualify a loan on the property's income commonly look for at least 1.0, and often 1.2 or higher, though the floor and the formula vary by lender. Break-even occupancy here is 112%: the yearly bills ($9,958 + $20,952 = $30,910) exceed a full year of rent, so even a tenant who never leaves does not cover them.

The underlying problem: the cap rate ($16,262 ÷ $320,000) is 5.1% and the loan costs 7.25%. When the cap rate sits below the loan's interest rate, borrowing generally lowers your cash return instead of raising it.

Side by side

  • Monthly cash flow: short-term makes $173, long-term loses $391, a $564 difference.
  • Cash needed: $91,600 short-term, $73,600 long-term. The $18,000 gap is the furnishing.
  • Room for error: about 4 points of occupancy short-term, none long-term.

The trade: income versus work, variability and regulation

  • Work. Guests need messages answered, cleanings scheduled and supplies restocked every week. A long-term tenant mostly needs a lease and the occasional repair. Hiring a full-service manager can push fees above the 15% assumed here.
  • Variability. While a tenant is in place, the $2,300 rent is the same every month. Nightly revenue moves with seasons, events and competing listings. The 60% is a yearly average, so some months may fall below break-even.
  • Regulation. Many cities, counties and HOAs restrict or ban short-term rentals, and rules can change after you buy. If that happens here, the fallback is the version that loses $391 a month.

What this comparison leaves out

  • Repair reserves. The long-term numbers set aside 10% of rent, $230 a month, for maintenance and CapEx. The short-term $950 has no such line. Reserve the same $230 and the $173 becomes a loss of $57 a month.
  • Furniture replacement. The $18,000 of furnishings wears out. Replacing it is not in these numbers.
  • Financing and insurance. Lenders and insurers often treat short-term rentals differently, so get quotes for the use you plan.
  • Taxes. Everything here is pre-tax. How each model is taxed is a question for a tax professional.

The honest read: at this price and rate, the long-term version loses money every month and the short-term version barely makes any. Buying it means betting on occupancy holding and local rules staying put.
